1. The business in one paragraph

Ship in a Bottle will import ready-to-drink cocktails with a large social-media following and no local availability, starting with round, 200 ml, 15% cocktails, and sell them online to adults in Cape Town and by the case to licensed bars, bottle stores and events. The brand is built as a multi-product importer: "the drinks you've seen online, landed here".

2. Why it can work

  • Demand exists before the first rand of advertising. The product is widely filmed and shared; people here search for where to buy it.
  • No local seller found. The brand's owner has launched in the UK, Ireland, Mexico and India. My research found no South African distributor.
  • A different product from local coolers. A 15% single-serve cocktail at about R99 sits apart from 5% ciders at R20.
  • Two channels. Online to the public for margin; cases to trade for volume.
  • A waiting list before launch. The website already collects sign-ups, so the first pallet has buyers before it lands.
  • Licences are a moat. The months of licensing that make this hard to start also make it hard to copy.

3. Why it might not

Stated plainly, so you can weigh it:

  • The licence may be delayed or refused. Nothing can be sold until it is granted.
  • The brand owner may appoint a large distributor, or object to independent imports. The shop would then pivot to other imported brands.
  • Margins are thin without distributor pricing. Buying from overseas wholesalers leaves about 24% gross margin; official supply about 41%.
  • The rand. Every cost is in foreign currency; every sale is in rand.
  • It may be a fad. The plan answers this by adding new imports each quarter.
  • The founder is new to the liquor trade and is running two other start-ups.

How the figures are worked. Half of each year's profit is paid out; a loss year pays nothing. Expected case, year 2: profit R260,000, payout R130,000, investor's 70% is R91,000. Strong case, year 2: payout R475,000; the investor still needs R282,000, which takes the first R403,000 of the payout at 70%; the remaining R72,000 splits 35/65, adding R25,000, for R307,000. Figures are rounded. Tax reduces all payouts. Founder's salary: cautious R0, R6,000 and R10,000 a month; expected R8,000, R15,000 and R22,000; strong R20,000, R25,000 and R30,000 on meeting agreed profit targets.

What separates the cases. The expected case stays on overseas-wholesaler pricing in year one and wins better terms from year two. The strong case has an official distribution agreement from the start. The cautious case never gets beyond wholesaler pricing and low volume.

7. Why this split is fair to both

  • The investor's risk is staged. Under a third of the money is exposed before the licence exists.
  • The investor is repaid at 70% of payouts, the fastest rate of the three businesses, reflecting the highest risk.
  • The founder takes no salary until there are sales, a low capped salary after, and earns the larger share only once the investor is whole.
  • The founder keeps 65% and control, because the licences, relationships and brand depend on one accountable person.
