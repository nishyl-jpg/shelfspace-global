Legal requirements and your ground work

Importing and selling alcohol sits under four layers of law at once: national liquor registration, a provincial liquor licence, customs and excise, and product and labelling rules. None is optional and they take months. Part A sets out each one. Part B is your plan, in stages, so that money is only spent when the step before it has succeeded.

This one needs specialists. Do not skip them. Liquor licensing is technical, and mistakes cost months. Before spending beyond stage 0:
  1. A liquor licensing attorney or consultant in the Western Cape to confirm which licences and registrations your exact model needs, and to run the applications.
  2. A customs clearing agent to classify each product, confirm duties and handle the import.
  3. An accountant for VAT, excise and stock records.
  4. An attorney for the supply or partner agreement and the shareholders' agreement.
Everything below is researched guidance to prepare you for those meetings, not legal advice. Several of my sources are older regulations; items tagged verify must be confirmed.

How the provincial application works

  1. Secure premises you have the right to occupy (a signed lease or option), with a floor plan.
  2. Your consultant prepares the application: forms, plan, description of the business, proof of zoning, your ID and company documents, and information showing you are a fit and proper person. A criminal record for certain offences can disqualify you ask the consultant.
  3. The application is lodged with the Authority and with the police's designated liquor officer by 14:00 on the last Friday of a month.
  4. A notice of the application must be displayed at the premises for 28 days. Neighbours, the police and the municipality may object.
  5. The Authority considers reports and objections and grants, refuses or sets conditions.
  6. On granting, you pay the fee and receive the licence. Only then may the website's licence setting be filled in.

Things that help: premises not near schools or places of worship; a storage-and-delivery model with no walk-in customers; a written plan for age checks on delivery.

A5. Premises, staff and insurance

  • Premises: lockable, alarmed unit with a caged stock area; cool and out of sunlight. Ask Environmental Health whether storing sealed drinks needs a certificate of acceptability verify.
  • Staff: everyone handling sales or delivery must be 18 or older. Minimum wage R30.23 an hour; UIF and Compensation Fund as for the other businesses.
  • Insurance: stock (theft, fire), goods in transit including the sea leg, public and product liability.

A6. If you start through a licensed partner (route B)

  • The partner, as licence holder, must be the seller: their name and licence number on invoices, stock dispatched from their licensed premises, payments received by them.
  • Ship in a Bottle earns a marketing and sales fee or commission under a written agreement.
  • The website must then show the partner's details and licence number as the seller.
  • Agree in writing: who owns the customer list (you), who owns the brand name (you), exclusivity, minimum volumes, how either side exits.

Attorney to structure this so that you are not selling liquor without a licence.

Part B · Your ground work, in stages

Each stage ends with a decision. Do not spend the next stage's money until the gate before it is passed.

Stage 0 · No-regret steps (start now; your own R11,000; about 6 weeks, alongside Mooimaak)

  • Register the company and the domain; secure the social handles. Publish the website in waiting-list mode on its own domain.
  • Book a one-hour paid consultation with a Western Cape liquor licensing attorney or consultant. Take Part A with you. Ask: which registrations does my model need; what will the whole process cost; what premises will pass; how long now?
  • Email the brand owner's international sales team (Sazerac). Ask plainly: do you have a South African distributor; if not, what do you require of one? Keep the reply, whatever it says.
  • List ten South African liquor importers and distributors. Phone five. Ask whether they would import the product with you as the online and marketing partner.
  • Ask two customs clearing agents for a written estimate: one pallet of 2,400 units, 200 ml, 15%, from the UK or USA to Cape Town, all duties and charges.
  • Get wholesale price lists from three overseas wholesalers.
  • Buy a few units through a lawful channel to taste, photograph and check the label against the requirements in A3.
  • Begin phase 1 content. Target 300 waiting-list sign-ups by the end of this stage.
Gate 0. Go on only if: (1) the consultant confirms a workable licensing path; (2) real landed cost is R62 a unit or less, or a partner offers at least R10 a unit; and (3) the brand owner has not told you to stop. If the brand owner refuses, switch the range to other imported brands and re-cost.

Stage 1 · Raise and apply (months 2 to 3)

  • If a licensed partner is willing: sign the partner agreement and begin selling through them. This is your proof for investors.
  • Update the investor proposal with real quotes and send it to people you know personally.
  • Sign the shareholders' agreement; receive tranche 1 (R120,000).
  • With the consultant, choose and lease correctly zoned premises. Sign only with a clause allowing you to exit if the licence is refused.
  • Fit alarm, cage and shelving.
  • Lodge the provincial licence application by the last Friday of the month. Display the notice for 28 days.
  • Lodge the national distributor registration.
  • Apply for the customs code and VAT number.

Stage 2 · Wait productively (months 3 to 7)

  • Answer every query from the authorities within two days.
  • Grow the waiting list to 1,500 and the trade list to 30 licensed venues.
  • Design the compliant label sticker; have the consultant check it.
  • Prepare import certificate applications for each product.
  • Negotiate supply terms; get a pro-forma invoice. Do not pay for stock yet.
  • Build the online shop on a hosted platform with age confirmation at checkout; keep it switched off.
  • Write the driver instructions and refusal log.
Gate 1. Tranche 2 (R330,000) is released only when the provincial licence is granted, the national registration is issued, and a supply agreement or confirmed order terms are in hand.

Stage 3 · First shipment and launch (months 7 to 9)

  • Pay for the first pallet. Clearing agent books freight and insurance.
  • Obtain import certificates; give the required notice before arrival.
  • Clear customs: duty, excise and import VAT paid. Apply stickers. Count stock into the cage.
  • Enter the licence number in the website settings block; the site switches from waiting list to ordering.
  • Message the waiting list first, then launch publicly (phase 2 content).
  • Deliver trade cases to the first ten licensed venues; collect a copy of each licence.
  • Order the second pallet when half the first is sold.

Stage 4 · First 90 days of trading

  • Weekly: stock count, units sold by channel, average price, landed cost, refusals logged.
  • Visit ten licensed venues a week.
  • Test the R99 price against R109.
  • With three months of sales figures, write to the brand owner again, with your licensed partner if you have one, and ask for official distribution terms.
  • Diarise licence renewal dates and annual returns for both authorities.

What only you can do

  • Sit in front of the licensing consultant, the partner importers and the investors
  • Be the named, fit and proper applicant on the licence
  • Sign the lease and take responsibility for the premises
  • Decide at each gate whether to go on, change course or stop
