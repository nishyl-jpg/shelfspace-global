1. The business in one paragraph

Groengeld delivers standing weekly fruit and veg orders to Cape Town households and small restaurants. Customers set their order once; Groengeld buys exactly that at the Cape Town Market at dawn and delivers the same morning. Because every item is sold before it is bought, spoilage and unsold stock, the usual killers of produce businesses, are kept small. Households pay in advance.

2. Why it works

  • Recurring revenue. A standing order repeats every week without being re-sold. Each new customer adds to a base that is still there next month.
  • Sold before bought. Purchases match confirmed orders, so stock risk is close to zero.
  • Cash comes in first. Households prepay; the business buys with customers' money.
  • An underserved customer. Small kitchens are too small for the large wholesalers to look after and currently send their own staff to the market.
  • Route economics. Each added customer on an existing route costs almost nothing extra to serve. Margin rises with density.
  • Everyone eats. Demand does not depend on fashion or season.

6. Why this split is fair to both

  • The investor's money buys hard assets that keep resale value, and the investor is first in line for payouts and for the assets if things go wrong.
  • The founder does the 04:30 starts, on a capped salary, and keeps control and 75% of the long-term value.
  • Half of profit is retained, so the second vehicle is funded by the business, not by diluting either party.
  • Monthly reporting on six plain numbers means the investor sees trouble early.

7. Alternative: asset finance plus a smaller raise

If you would rather give up less of the company:

  • Finance the vehicle through a bank or dealer (needs a deposit and trading history verify what lenders require).
  • Raise R170,000 for cold room, packing station, working capital and marketing, for 12.5% on the same repaid-first terms.
  • Trade-off: monthly instalments of roughly R5,500 verify come out of profit whether the month is good or bad.

8. Risks an investor should weigh

  • Thin margins. About 28% gross. Small slips in buying or spoilage matter. Mitigation: weekly tracking of produce cost and credits; household boxes flex contents to hold margin.
  • Restaurant credit. Restaurants fail often. Mitigation: cash on delivery for four weeks, 7-day terms, stop-supply rule, owner surety, 15% cap per customer.
  • Founder's time is shared with another business. Mitigation: a hired driver-packer runs mornings from stage 2; the founder manages buying and sales.
  • Competition from supermarket delivery and large wholesalers. Mitigation: standing-order convenience, same-morning market freshness, personal service to small accounts.
  • Vehicle dependence. Mitigation: insurance, hire-vehicle fallback, second vehicle from retained profit.
  • Food safety incident. Mitigation: whole produce only, certificate of acceptability, traceable slips, product liability insurance.

9. What I am asking you for

R350,000, a monthly look at six numbers, and introductions to restaurant owners, school bursars and estate managers. In return: first claim on payouts until you are repaid, security over the vehicle and cold room, and 25% of a business built on weekly recurring orders.
